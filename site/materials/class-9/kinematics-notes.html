01 Rest, motion and reference frames

Kinematics describes motion without investigating its causes. An object is at rest when its position stays unchanged relative to a chosen reference point. It is in motion when its position changes relative to that reference point with time. Always identify the reference frame before describing motion.

A passenger sitting in a moving bus is at rest relative to the seat but moving relative to a roadside tree. These descriptions are consistent because they use different reference points. A reference point may itself be moving.

Check: Two passengers sitting beside each other have zero relative velocity if their separation and direction remain unchanged, even though the bus is moving along the road.

02 Translatory motion: linear, circular and random

In translatory motion a body moves from one place to another. Linear motion follows a straight path, such as a train along a straight track. Circular motion follows a circular path, such as a stone moving around a hand at the end of a string. Random motion follows an irregular path with changing directions, such as a butterfly's flight.

Classify the path and specify the object being observed. A body can have more than one type of motion at the same time: a rolling wheel moves along the road while also turning about its axle.

Distinction: Circular motion around an external centre differs from the rotation of a body about its own axis.

03 Rotatory and vibratory motion

Rotatory motion occurs when a body turns about an axis. A ceiling fan rotates about its fixed central axis; points on its blades travel in circles. Vibratory motion is repeated to-and-fro motion about a mean position. A pendulum and a vibrating tuning fork provide examples.

In a pendulum's vibration, its direction repeatedly reverses. The pendulum can have zero speed momentarily at an extreme position without being permanently at rest. To describe a combination of motions, state each part separately rather than forcing the object into only one category.

Recall: A spinning fan is rotatory; an oscillating pendulum is vibratory; irregular insect flight is random.

04 Distance and displacement

Distance is the total length of the path travelled. It is a scalar and has no direction. Displacement is the vector from the initial position to the final position, independent of the route taken. Both are measured in metres in SI. The magnitude of displacement cannot exceed the distance travelled.

Distance = sum of path lengths
Displacement = final position − initial position

Example: Walking 24 m east and 7 m west gives distance 24 + 7 = 31 m. With east positive, displacement is 24 − 7 = 17 m east. After one complete lap, displacement is zero although the distance is the track circumference.

05 Scalars, vectors and arrow representation

A scalar is specified by magnitude and unit. Examples include mass, time, temperature, distance and speed. A vector requires magnitude and direction; examples include displacement, velocity, acceleration and force.

Draw a vector as an arrow. Its length represents magnitude according to a selected scale, and its arrowhead shows direction. At a scale of 1 cm to 2 m, a displacement of 6 m east is represented by a 3 cm arrow pointing east. Changing the drawing scale does not change the actual displacement.

Check: A negative sign may indicate direction along a selected axis, but merely having a sign does not make every quantity a vector.

06 Speed and average speed

Speed describes how quickly distance is covered. Uniform speed means equal distances are travelled in equal time intervals. For a journey with changing speed, calculate average speed using the entire distance and entire elapsed time, including any stated stops.

Average speed = total distance / total time
SI unit: m/s

Example: A cyclist covers 90 m in 15 s and 60 m in 10 s. Average speed = (90 + 60)/(15 + 10) = 6 m/s.

Do not automatically average two speeds. Their arithmetic mean applies when the time spent at each speed is equal. For equal distances at 10 m/s and 20 m/s, the average is 13.3 m/s.

07 Velocity and changes of direction

Velocity specifies the rate of change of displacement and its direction. Average velocity equals net displacement divided by the elapsed time. Constant velocity requires both constant speed and constant direction. A car turning around a circle changes velocity even if its speed stays constant.

Average velocity = displacement / time interval

Example: A runner completes a 200 m lap in 40 s. Average speed = 200/40 = 5 m/s, but average velocity is zero because the runner returns to the starting point.

Instantaneous speed or velocity describes motion at a particular instant. An average describes an interval and need not equal the value at every instant.

08 Units and speed conversions

Use compatible units before substituting into an equation. The SI units of distance, time, velocity and acceleration are m, s, m/s and m/s². Convert kilometres to metres and hours to seconds when working with SI acceleration.

1 km = 1,000 m
1 h = 3,600 s
1 km/h = 5/18 m/s
1 m/s = 3.6 km/h

Example: 72 km/h = 72 × 5/18 = 20 m/s. A speed of 8 m/s maintained for 5 s covers 8 × 5 = 40 m.

Unit check: (m/s) × s gives m; (m/s)/s gives m/s². A result with the wrong unit indicates an incorrect calculation or interpretation.

09 Acceleration and uniform acceleration

Acceleration is the change in velocity per unit time. Uniform acceleration means equal changes in velocity occur in equal time intervals. It can result from changing speed, changing direction, or both. If velocity is constant, acceleration is zero.

a = (v − u)/t

Here u is initial velocity, v final velocity and t the elapsed time. A velocity increase from 4 m/s to 10 m/s in 3 s gives a = (10 − 4)/3 = 2 m/s².

The equation gives average acceleration over the interval. It also describes the acceleration throughout the interval if acceleration is constant.

10 Signs of velocity and acceleration

Choose a positive direction before using signed quantities. A negative velocity means motion opposite to that direction. A negative acceleration points in the negative direction; it does not always mean the object is slowing down.

Slowing: With east positive, changing from +18 m/s to +6 m/s in 4 s gives a = −3 m/s². Velocity and acceleration are opposite, so speed decreases.

Speeding up: Changing from −4 m/s to −10 m/s in 3 s gives a = −2 m/s². The speed increases from 4 to 10 m/s because velocity and acceleration point in the same direction.

Use the magnitude of velocity when comparing speeds.

11 Reading distance–time graphs

Place time on the horizontal axis and distance on the vertical axis. The gradient gives speed: change in distance divided by change in time. A steeper rising line represents a greater speed. A straight rising line represents uniform speed; a horizontal line means the distance is unchanged and the object is at rest.

Speed = gradient = Δdistance/Δtime

Example: A line rises from 10 m at 2 s to 50 m at 10 s. Speed = (50 − 10)/(10 − 2) = 5 m/s.

Use two points on the line and subtract both coordinates. Do not divide the final distance by the final time unless the graph starts at the origin.

12 Speed–time graphs: gradients and areas

On a speed–time graph, the gradient gives the rate of change of speed. For straight-line motion without direction reversal, it can represent acceleration with the chosen direction convention. A horizontal line indicates constant speed. An upward gradient indicates increasing speed and a downward gradient decreasing speed.

The area under a speed–time graph gives distance travelled. A rectangle has area speed × time. A triangle has area ½ × base × height. Add areas for a journey with several stages.

Example: Speed rises uniformly from 0 to 12 m/s in 6 s. Distance = ½ × 6 × 12 = 36 m. Gradient = 12/6 = 2 m/s².

13 The three equations of uniformly accelerated motion

The standard equations apply when acceleration is constant over the interval. Use a consistent positive direction. The symbol s represents displacement; when the body continues in one direction, its magnitude equals the distance travelled.

v = u + at
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as

The first equation follows from a = (v − u)/t. Under constant acceleration, average velocity is (u + v)/2. Multiplying by time gives s = ½(u + v)t, from which the second and third forms follow.

Select the equation containing the known quantities and the required unknown. Do not assume constant acceleration merely because a journey is described by one time interval.

14 Worked acceleration and braking problems

Acceleration: For u = 5 m/s, a = 2 m/s² and t = 4 s, final velocity v = 5 + 2 × 4 = 13 m/s. Displacement s = 5 × 4 + ½ × 2 × 4² = 36 m.

Braking: A car moving at 20 m/s brakes uniformly at 5 m/s². Take forward as positive, so a = −5 m/s² and v = 0. Time to stop is (0 − 20)/(−5) = 4 s. From v² = u² + 2as, stopping distance is 40 m.

Method: List the data, convert units, choose signs, substitute into a suitable equation and check the result physically. A negative stopping time usually means the acceleration sign was used incorrectly.

15 Free fall and acceleration due to gravity

Free fall is motion under gravity alone, with air resistance neglected. Near Earth's surface the gravitational acceleration is approximately 9.8 m/s². Some school problems use 10 m/s²; use the value stated in the question. Objects in the same gravitational field have the same free-fall acceleration when air resistance is negligible.

For downward motion with downward chosen positive, a = +g. If upward is chosen positive, a = −g. The physical direction of gravity remains downward in either convention.

Example: A stone released from rest for 3 s, using g = 10 m/s², has downward speed gt = 30 m/s and falls ½gt² = 45 m.

16 Vertical upward motion and final checks

A ball thrown upward slows because its upward velocity and downward acceleration are opposite. At the highest point its velocity is momentarily zero, but its acceleration remains downward. It then falls back with increasing downward speed. Neglecting air resistance, it returns to the launch height with the same speed magnitude.

Example: With upward positive, u = 20 m/s and g = 10 m/s². At the top, 0 = 20 − 10t, giving t = 2 s. From 0 = 20² − 2 × 10 × h, maximum height is 20 m. Return time to the same height is 4 s.

Check the chosen direction, gravitational value, constant-acceleration assumption, units, and whether the question asks for distance or displacement.
