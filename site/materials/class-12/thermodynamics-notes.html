01 Heat, temperature and thermal work

Thermodynamics describes energy transfer and the changes in a system's macroscopic state. Heat and work are two ways energy crosses a system boundary; neither is a substance stored in the system.

Heat Q is energy transfer caused by a temperature difference. In ordinary spontaneous thermal contact, it flows from higher to lower temperature. Temperature describes the thermal state; it is not the total energy of a body.

Work transfers energy mechanically or through other organized interactions. Rubbing your hands converts mechanical energy to internal energy. Compressing gas can warm it even when no heat enters.

QuantityMeaningSI unit
HeatEnergy transferred because of temperature differenceJ
WorkEnergy transferred by an interaction such as a moving pistonJ
TemperatureA measure of thermal stateK

An object contains internal energy; it does not contain “heat” as a state property. Heat refers to the transfer process.

02 System, surroundings and boundary

A system is the part of the physical universe chosen for study. Everything outside it is the surroundings. A real or imaginary boundary separates the two. Together the system and surroundings form the universe under consideration.

For gas in a cylinder, choose the gas as the system. The piston and nearby air then belong to its surroundings. The boundary can move when the piston moves.

Why the choice matters: The gas's expansion work leaves the gas system and enters the surroundings. If gas and piston are both included inside a larger system, the transfer between them is internal to that larger system.

Define the system and the signs before substituting numbers. A boundary that prevents matter crossing can still permit heat or work to cross.

03 Open, closed and isolated systems
SystemMatter exchangeEnergy exchangeExample
OpenAllowedAllowedUncovered hot drink losing vapour and energy
ClosedNot allowedAllowedSealed gas cylinder with a movable piston
IsolatedNot allowedNot allowedIdeal sealed, insulated, rigid container

A closed system can exchange work as well as heat. An insulated wall prevents heat transfer, but a movable piston may still allow work. Therefore “insulated” does not by itself mean “isolated.”

A real thermos approximates isolation but is not perfectly isolated. Its contents may slowly exchange energy with the environment.

Do not classify a movable, insulated piston as isolated merely because Q = 0. Work can still cross its boundary.

04 Deriving pressure–volume work

Consider gas pushing a piston of area A through a small outward distance s against constant external pressure Pext.

F = PextA
W = Fs = PextAs
ΔV = As
W = Pext(Vf − Vi)

This page uses W positive for work done by the gas. Expansion gives ΔV > 0 and W > 0. Compression gives ΔV < 0 and W < 0. Work done on the gas is Won = −W.

Example: At Pext = 100 kPa, a gas expands from 2 L to 5 L. ΔV = 3 × 10⁻³ m³, so W = 100000 × 0.003 = 300 J.

Use a single constant P only when the external pressure is constant. Gas pressure equals external pressure approximately for a quasistatic process, not every expansion.

05 PV diagrams, areas and units

For quasistatic expansion, work done by the gas is the signed area under its pressure–volume curve.

W = ∫ P dV
At constant pressure: W = PΔV

A horizontal line on a PV diagram is constant pressure. A vertical line has constant volume and zero PV work. Expansion proceeds to the right and gives positive work; compression proceeds to the left and gives negative work.

06 Changing pressure, paths and free expansion

If pressure changes linearly from Pi to Pf during a quasistatic volume change, the PV area is a trapezium:

W = ½(Pi + Pf)(Vf − Vi)

Example: Pressure falls linearly from 300 kPa to 100 kPa while volume grows from 1 L to 3 L. W = ½(300 + 100)(3 − 1) = 400 J. Using the initial pressure alone would incorrectly give 600 J.

Different paths between the same states can give different work. Therefore work is a path-dependent transfer, not a state function.

Free expansion: If gas expands against vacuum, Pext = 0, so its boundary work is zero even though its volume increases. If it is also insulated and no other work occurs, Q = 0 and ΔU = 0. An ideal gas then keeps the same temperature after equilibrium is restored.

07 Internal energy and temperature

Internal energy U is the sum of the microscopic kinetic and potential energies associated with particles in a system. It excludes the kinetic energy of the body's bulk motion and its external gravitational potential energy.

For a fixed amount and composition of ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature. A temperature increase gives a positive ΔU. At constant temperature, changing the ideal gas's pressure or volume alone does not change U.

ΔU = nCVΔT
Ideal monatomic gas: U = 3nRT/2
Mean translational energy per particle = 3kT/2

The monatomic formula assumes only translational degrees of freedom contribute in the temperature range considered. The per-particle energy 3kT/2 must not be confused with total sample energy.

For real substances, intermolecular potential energy matters. Energy can change during a phase change at constant temperature, so “constant temperature means ΔU = 0” must be restricted to a fixed ideal-gas sample.

08 First law and sign conventions

The first law applies conservation of energy to a thermodynamic system. For a closed system with negligible bulk kinetic/potential energy changes:

ΔU = Q − W
Q = ΔU + W
  • Q > 0: heat enters the system.
  • Q < 0: heat leaves the system.
  • W > 0: the system does work on its surroundings.
  • W < 0: the surroundings do work on the system.

If work done on the system is used instead, write ΔU = Q + Won. These conventions describe the same physics; never switch signs halfway through a solution.

Worked example: A gas absorbs 500 J and does 180 J of work. ΔU = 500 − 180 = 320 J. It retains the energy not transferred out as work.

Compression example: A gas loses 80 J of heat while 200 J of work is done on it. Q = −80 J and W = −200 J, so ΔU = −80 − (−200) = 120 J.

09 State functions, cycles and energy balances

Internal energy is a state function. Its change is fixed by the initial and final states. Heat and work depend on the path, but their difference Q − W must equal the same ΔU for paths connecting those states.

Example: Between the same two states, path A transfers Q = 500 J and W = 200 J, giving ΔU = 300 J. If path B involves W = 80 J, it must transfer Q = 300 + 80 = 380 J.

Complete cycle: ΔU = 0
Qnet = Wnet

A cycle returns to the initial thermodynamic state; individual stages can still exchange nonzero heat and work. For a quasistatic PV cycle, the enclosed signed area gives net work: clockwise is positive and anticlockwise negative under the by-gas convention.

An isolated system exchanges neither heat nor work, so its total energy remains constant. Internal transfers within it do not create or destroy energy.

10 Isochoric and isobaric processes
ProcessFixed quantityPV workFirst-law result
IsochoricVolumeW = 0Q = ΔU
IsobaricPressureW = PΔVQ = ΔU + PΔV

In a rigid container, gas cannot move the boundary, so no pressure–volume work occurs. Heating at constant volume increases the internal energy and, for fixed ideal gas, raises its temperature and pressure.

At constant pressure, heat can both raise internal energy and support expansion work. Not all the supplied heat becomes work.

Example: A gas absorbs 700 J while expanding by 2 L at 100 kPa. W = 100 × 2 = 200 J. Thus ΔU = 700 − 200 = 500 J.

W = 0 for an isochoric process refers to PV boundary work. A rigid system can still receive other work, such as electrical energy or stirring; the simple Q = ΔU case excludes those inputs.

11 Isothermal ideal-gas processes

An isothermal process has constant temperature. For a fixed ideal-gas sample, this gives ΔU = 0, so:

Q = W
PV = nRT = constant

During isothermal expansion, the gas does positive work and must absorb an equal amount of heat to maintain its temperature. During compression, W < 0 and Q < 0: the surroundings supply work and the gas rejects heat.

A slow expansion in thermal contact with a large reservoir can approximate this process. A constant-temperature process is not necessarily adiabatic.

For a reversible isothermal expansion, integrating P = nRT/V gives:

W = nRT ln(Vf/Vi)

Example: An ideal gas does 450 J of work isothermally. It absorbs 450 J of heat and has ΔU = 0. For fixed temperature, doubling its volume halves its pressure.

12 Adiabatic processes and comparison

An adiabatic process transfers no heat: Q = 0. Insulation or a sufficiently rapid process can approximate it.

ΔU = −W

In adiabatic expansion, W > 0, so ΔU < 0: an ideal gas cools. In adiabatic compression, W < 0, so ΔU > 0: an ideal gas warms. “No heat transfer” does not mean no temperature change.

Example: 240 J of work is done on an insulated gas. W = −240 J, giving ΔU = 240 J. If its total constant-volume heat capacity is 12 J/K, the temperature rises by 20 K.

For a reversible adiabatic ideal-gas process with constant heat-capacity ratio γ:

PVγ = constant   γ = CP/CV

This relation needs the stated assumptions and does not apply to every irreversible or free expansion. The chapter's core result Q = 0, ΔU = −W does not require reversibility.

13 Heat capacity and specific heat

Heat capacity Cbody describes the energy needed to raise the temperature of a particular body by one kelvin under specified conditions. It depends on the amount of material.

Cbody = Q/ΔT   unit J/K
Q = mcΔT   Cbody = mc

Specific heat capacity c is per unit mass, with SI unit J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. For the same material, phase, temperature range and process, c does not depend on sample mass. Heat capacity mc doubles when sample mass doubles.

A temperature difference of 1 °C equals 1 K. For example, heating from 20 °C to 45 °C gives ΔT = 25 K. Do not add 273 to a temperature difference.

Use Q = mcΔT when the specified c is appropriate and no phase change occurs. If c varies appreciably with temperature, integration or an appropriate average is required.

14 Heating calculations and calorimetry

Heating water: For 0.20 kg water warmed by 20 K with c = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, Q = 0.20 × 4200 × 20 = 16800 J. If mass is given in grams, convert it or use a compatible per-gram c.

Cooling: A 0.50 kg body with c = 400 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ cools from 80 °C to 30 °C. Q = 0.50 × 400 × (30 − 80) = −10000 J. The negative sign means heat leaves it; heat lost has magnitude 10000 J.

Calorimetry: If the combined system is thermally isolated and the calorimeter's heat capacity is negligible:

Heat lost by hot body = heat gained by cold body
mhch(Th − Tf) = mccc(Tf − Tc)

For 100 g and 200 g water initially at 80 °C and 20 °C, the same c cancels: 100(80 − Tf) = 200(Tf − 20), so Tf = 40 °C. State negligible heat loss, no phase change, and negligible container capacity.

15 Molar heat capacity at constant volume and pressure
Cm = Q/(nΔT)   unit J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹
Q = nCmΔT
Cbody = nCm = mc
Cm = Mc

Molar heat capacity is per mole, while mass-specific heat capacity is per kilogram. Molar mass M must be in kg/mol if c is in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

For a fixed ideal gas heated with only PV work, at constant volume QV = nCVΔT = ΔU. At constant pressure QP = nCPΔT, because energy also supplies expansion work.

Example: Two moles absorb 1500 J while their temperature rises 25 K. The molar capacity for the specified process is 1500/(2 × 25) = 30 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. The total sample capacity is 60 J/K.

16 Deriving Cp minus Cv and solving ideal-gas problems

For one mole at constant pressure, start with Q = ΔU + W. Use Q = CPΔT, ΔU = CVΔT, and PΔV = RΔT from the ideal-gas equation.

CPΔT = CVΔT + RΔT
CP − CV = R

R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ when rounded for calculations. The n-mole total capacity difference is nR. For molar capacities the difference is R regardless of sample size.

γ = CP/CV
CV = R/(γ − 1)
CP = γR/(γ − 1)

In the ideal monatomic translational model, CV = 3R/2, CP = 5R/2, and γ = 5/3. Other gases can have different values because other degrees of freedom contribute.

Worked example: One mole has CV = 20 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and warms by 10 K at constant pressure. ΔU = 200 J; W = RΔT = 83.1 J; Q = 283.1 J. Thus CP = 28.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
