01 Production of sound

Sound is produced by a vibrating source. A tuning fork's prongs, a drum membrane or a loudspeaker cone move to and fro and set nearby particles of the medium into vibration. The disturbance then transfers energy through the medium.

Particles oscillate about their mean positions; they do not all travel permanently from the source to the listener. A vibrating source alone does not guarantee that a distant listener hears it: a transmitting medium and a suitable audible frequency and intensity are also needed.

Example: Touching a gently vibrating tuning fork can show its vibration. The vibration of a nearby surface can also transfer sound energy to air.

02 Longitudinal sound waves

Sound in air is a longitudinal mechanical wave. Mechanical means it requires a material medium. Longitudinal means the particles vibrate parallel to the direction in which the wave travels. A sequence of compressions and rarefactions moves away from the source.

The disturbance transfers energy even though individual air particles make small back-and-forth movements rather than following the wave over a large distance. A wave diagram drawn as a curve may show pressure or displacement variation; the drawing does not mean that air particles move up and down as in a transverse wave.

Recall: Sound in air is longitudinal; light is an electromagnetic wave and can travel through vacuum.

03 Compressions, rarefactions and wavelength

A compression is a region where particles are closer together and pressure and density are higher than in the neighbouring undisturbed medium. A rarefaction is a region where particles are farther apart, with lower pressure and density. A rarefaction still contains particles; it is not a vacuum.

One wavelength is the distance between consecutive compressions or between consecutive rarefactions. These positions correspond to the same stage of vibration. The distance from a compression to its neighbouring rarefaction is half a wavelength.

As a source vibrates, it alternately pushes and releases the nearby medium, creating the repeating pattern. Wavelength is measured in metres.

04 Why sound needs a medium

Sound travels through gases, liquids and solids because their particles can transfer mechanical disturbances. It cannot travel through a vacuum. In the bell-jar demonstration, a ringing electric bell becomes harder to hear as air is removed, even though the bell is still seen vibrating.

The demonstration distinguishes mechanical sound from light: light from the bell can still reach the observer through the evacuated space. In an actual apparatus, some vibration may also pass through solid supports, so a faint residual sound does not disprove the need for a medium.

Check: Removing air reduces transmission; it does not change sound into light or remove gravity.

05 Frequency, period and wave speed

Frequency f is the number of complete vibrations per second, measured in hertz. Period T is the time for one complete vibration, measured in seconds. Sound travels one wavelength λ during one period.

f = 1/T
v = λ/T = fλ

Example: A period of 0.002 s gives f = 500 Hz. If wavelength is 0.68 m, speed is 500 × 0.68 = 340 m/s.

In the same medium under unchanged conditions, speed is approximately fixed. Doubling frequency therefore halves wavelength. Do not confuse frequency, which is set by the source, with the speed determined by the medium and its conditions.

06 Speed of sound in different media

The speed of sound depends on the medium's elastic and inertial properties. For the representative examples in the chapter, sound travels fastest in steel, more slowly in water and slowest in air. This is a useful comparison for those materials, rather than a universal rule based only on whether matter is solid, liquid or gas.

Sound speed in air is often taken as about 340 m/s for school calculations. It varies with temperature and other conditions. Water problems may specify 1,500 m/s. Always use the value stated in the question.

Changing a source's loudness or frequency does not by itself greatly change the propagation speed in the same ordinary medium.

07 Measuring sound speed directly

Measure the distance between a sound source and an observer. The observer uses a visible event, such as a clap, as the start and the arrival of its sound as the end. Light travels much faster than sound, so its travel time over ordinary classroom distances is negligible.

Sound speed = distance / sound travel time

Example: Sound covers 170 m in 0.50 s, giving v = 170/0.50 = 340 m/s.

A longer separation makes the sound delay easier to measure, reducing the relative influence of reaction-time uncertainty. Repeat measurements where possible. This method uses a one-way path, so do not multiply the distance by two.

08 Temperature and humidity

For air of fixed composition, sound speed increases as temperature rises. The chapter models the dependence using the square root of absolute temperature. When applying this relation, temperatures must be in kelvin, not degrees Celsius.

v₂/v₁ = √(T₂/T₁)
T in kelvin = temperature in °C + 273 approximately

If absolute temperature becomes four times as large under the model's assumptions, sound speed doubles. At the same temperature, more humid air generally transmits sound slightly faster because its composition changes.

Do not assume speed is directly proportional to Celsius temperature, or apply an air-temperature formula to water without justification.

09 Pitch and frequency

Pitch distinguishes high and low sounds and is mainly determined by frequency. A larger frequency produces a higher pitch. Loudness and pitch are different: increasing vibration amplitude can make a sound louder without raising its frequency.

On oscilloscope traces using the same time scale, the wave with more complete cycles in a given interval has greater frequency and hence higher pitch. If trace A shows eight cycles and trace B four during the same interval, A has twice B's frequency.

Check: Compare the time scale before comparing cycle counts. Traces with different time scales cannot be compared by appearance alone.

10 Loudness and sound intensity

Loudness is the listener's perception of how strong a sound is. Under the same listening conditions, a greater vibration amplitude generally produces a louder sound. Sound intensity is an objective physical quantity: sound power transferred per unit area normal to propagation.

I = P/A
SI unit of intensity: W/m²

Example: If 0.60 W passes through 3.0 m², intensity is 0.60/3.0 = 0.20 W/m². Loudness level is commonly expressed in decibels.

Keep amplitude, frequency and wave speed distinct. A large amplitude does not necessarily mean a high-pitched sound, and equal intensity does not guarantee identical perceived loudness at different frequencies.

11 Quality or timbre and waveforms

Quality, also called timbre, allows us to distinguish sounds from different instruments even when they have the same pitch and similar loudness. Different sources produce different waveforms and mixtures of overtones.

A violin and another instrument can play the same note, so their fundamental frequencies are the same, while the shapes of their waveforms differ. An oscilloscope can display these differences. Frequency determines the repetition rate; amplitude relates to signal strength; waveform shape helps distinguish quality.

Check: If two traces have the same repetition rate and amplitude but different shapes, their timbre differs rather than their pitch necessarily differing.

12 Noise, musical sound and noise control

Noise is unwanted sound. The same sound can be welcome in one situation and disturbing in another. Musical sounds often involve regular repeating vibrations, whereas many noises have irregular waveforms. Whether a sound is unwanted also depends on the listener and circumstances.

Traffic, horns, machinery and loudspeakers can contribute to noise pollution. Reduce noise at the source through maintenance and quieter operation, along its path through suitable barriers and insulation, or by increasing separation where practical.

Prolonged excessive noise can interfere with communication and concentration and can damage hearing. A sound is not safe merely because it is musical or pleasant.

13 Reflection, echoes and minimum distance

An echo is reflected sound heard separately from the original sound after a sufficient delay. A reflecting wall or other large surface sends sound back to the listener. The measured delay includes both the outward and return journeys.

Reflector distance d = vt/2

Example: An echo returning after 0.40 s at 340 m/s comes from a surface 340 × 0.40/2 = 68 m away. If a separate echo requires at least 0.10 s delay, the minimum wall distance at 340 m/s is 17 m.

For a wall 51 m away, the round trip is 102 m and echo time is 102/340 = 0.30 s.

14 Audible sound, infrasound and ultrasound

The conventional audible range for a young person with normal hearing is approximately 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Actual hearing varies between people and with age. Sound below 20 Hz is called infrasound; sound above 20,000 Hz is ultrasound.

These classifications depend on frequency, not amplitude. Making ultrasound louder does not bring its frequency into the ordinary human audible range. Different animals have different hearing ranges; bats can use ultrasonic sound in locating prey.

Example: At v = 340 m/s and λ = 0.0085 m, frequency is v/λ = 40,000 Hz. This is ultrasound. For MHz values, convert 1 MHz to 1,000,000 Hz before using v = fλ.

15 SONAR and industrial ultrasound

SONAR uses transmitted sound pulses and returning echoes to locate underwater objects or measure water depth. Measure the round-trip time and use the sound speed in water; divide by two to obtain the one-way distance.

Example: A seabed echo returns after 0.20 s at 1,500 m/s. Depth = 1,500 × 0.20/2 = 150 m. Depths corresponding to 0.12 s and 0.20 s are 90 m and 150 m, a difference of 60 m.

Industrially, reflected ultrasound can reveal internal cracks or flaws in a solid block. Ultrasound in a cleaning liquid also helps detach dirt from small or inaccessible spaces. These applications use mechanical vibrations and reflection.

16 Medical ultrasound and solving chapter problems

Ultrasonography forms images using echoes from tissue boundaries. A transducer sends and receives ultrasonic pulses. Their travel times and the assumed speed in tissue help locate reflecting structures. Echocardiography uses ultrasound to form images of the heart and its valves.

Example: At 1,540 m/s, an echo time of 0.00010 s corresponds to depth 1,540 × 0.00010/2 = 0.077 m = 7.7 cm.

Problem method: Decide whether the path is one-way or a round trip, convert frequency and time units, choose v = fλ or d = vt as appropriate, and check units. An echo or SONAR depth uses vt/2; a direct sound journey uses vt. Explain the physical reason for the factor of two.
