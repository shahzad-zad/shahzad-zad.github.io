01 Scalars, vectors & representation

Kinematics describes motion without investigating its causes. Before describing motion, distinguish quantities that need a direction from those that do not.

Scalar and vector quantities
ScalarVector
Magnitude with an appropriate unitMagnitude and direction; follows vector addition
Mass, time, temperature, distance, speed, workDisplacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum

Represent a vector by a bold letter or an arrow over its symbol. Graphically, draw an arrow: length represents magnitude to a chosen scale; the arrow points in the vector's direction. The initial end is the tail; the terminal end is the head.

Example: At a scale of 1 cm to 2 N, a 6 N force east is a 3 cm arrow pointing east. Changing the scale changes the drawing, not the physical force.

A negative scalar is not a vector merely because it has a sign. Vector direction carries spatial information.

02 Cartesian coordinates & types of vectors

The Cartesian system uses mutually perpendicular x, y, and z axes meeting at an origin. A point P has coordinates (x, y, z), which specify its position relative to that origin.

  • Equal vectors: same magnitude and direction, even if drawn at different positions.
  • Negative vectors: A and −A have equal magnitudes and opposite directions.
  • Zero (null) vector: magnitude zero; no unique direction. A + (−A) = 0.
  • Unit vector: magnitude 1, indicating direction. For nonzero A, â = A/|A|. It is dimensionless.
  • Free vector: may be translated without changing magnitude or direction.
  • Position vector: points from the origin to a specified point.
r = x i + y j + z k
|r| = √(x² + y² + z²)

i, j, k are unit vectors along +x, +y, +z. For P(3, 4, 0) m, r = 3i + 4j m and |r| = 5 m. The unit vector along r is 0.6i + 0.8j.

A unit vector is undefined for the zero vector because division by its zero magnitude is impossible.

03 Vector addition, subtraction & scalar multiplication

Head-to-tail: place the tail of each new vector at the previous vector's head. The resultant runs from the first tail to the last head. For the parallelogram method, draw both vectors from a common tail; the diagonal from that tail is their sum.

A + B = B + A
A + (B + C) = (A + B) + C
A − B = A + (−B)

Vector addition is commutative and associative. To subtract B, reverse B and add it. Multiplication by a positive number scales magnitude without reversing direction; multiplication by a negative number also reverses direction.

If θ is the angle between vectors drawn from a common tail:

R = √(A² + B² + 2AB cos θ)
|A − B| ≤ R ≤ A + B

Example: Two 5 N forces have resultant 10 N if parallel, zero if opposite, and 5√2 N if perpendicular. The sum of magnitudes alone does not determine the resultant.

The minus sign in some triangle forms of the cosine rule uses an interior triangle angle. For the common-tail angle θ, the resultant formula above has +2AB cos θ.

04 Resolution & addition using components

Resolution splits a vector into perpendicular components. Measure θ from the positive x axis unless the question states another convention.

Ax = A cos θ   Ay = A sin θ
A = Ax i + Ay j

The components are signed. In quadrant II, x is negative and y positive. At 90°, the x component is zero. Add vectors by adding their components separately:

Rx = ΣAx   Ry = ΣAy
|R| = √(Rx² + Ry²)
tan θ = Ry/Rx

Worked example: A = (3i + 4j) N and B = (−i + 2j) N. R = (2i + 6j) N; |R| = √40 ≈ 6.32 N. Its direction is arctan(6/2) ≈ 71.6° above +x.

Direction check: For R = −3i + 3j, a calculator may return −45° from a simple inverse tangent. The vector is in quadrant II, so its angle measured anticlockwise from +x is 135°. Use quadrant reasoning or atan2.

05 Scalar product (dot product)
A · B = AB cos θ
A · B = AxBx + AyBy + AzBz

The dot product is a scalar. Geometrically, it multiplies one vector's magnitude by the component of the other along it. It is commutative and distributes over addition.

AngleResult
0°+AB
90°0
180°−AB

i·i = j·j = k·k = 1; mixed unit-vector dot products are zero. Also A·A = A². For nonzero vectors, cos θ = (A·B)/(AB).

Worked example: A = 2i + 3j − k and B = i − 2j + 4k. A·B = 2(1) + 3(−2) + (−1)(4) = −8. Their dot product is negative, so their angle is obtuse.

Application: Work by a constant force is W = F·s = Fs cos θ. A 10 N force at 60° to a 2 m displacement does 10 J of work.

06 Vector product (cross product)
|A × B| = AB sin θ
A × B = (AyBz − AzBy)i
+ (AzBx − AxBz)j
+ (AxBy − AyBx)k

The result is perpendicular to both vectors. Curl the fingers of the right hand from A toward B through the smaller angle; the thumb points along A×B. Reversing the order reverses direction: B×A = −(A×B).

i×j = k, j×k = i, k×i = j. Reversing a cyclic order gives the negative. Cross products of a unit vector with itself are zero. Parallel and antiparallel vectors have zero cross product; perpendicular vectors give magnitude AB.

The cross product distributes over addition, but is not generally associative. Its magnitude equals the area of the parallelogram spanned by the two vectors; half that magnitude is the triangle area.

Example: (2i)×(3j) = 6k. Torque is τ = r×F, so a 5 N force perpendicular to a 2 m lever arm produces torque of magnitude 10 N m. This chapter uses torque as a vector-product example, rather than a full equilibrium unit.

07 Position, displacement, distance & reference frame

Position is specified relative to an origin and reference frame. An object can be at rest in one frame and moving in another: a seated bus passenger is at rest relative to the bus but moving relative to the road.

Δr = rfinal − rinitial

Displacement is the vector change in position. Distance is the scalar length of the actual path. Distance is never less than the magnitude of displacement. They are equal for a straight path without reversal.

Worked example: Travel 8 m east and then 3 m west. Distance = 11 m; displacement = 5 m east. If the journey takes 5 s, average speed is 11/5 = 2.2 m/s and average velocity is 5/5 = 1 m/s east.

Relative motion in one dimension: Velocity of A relative to B is vA/B = vA − vB. If two vehicles travel east at 15 m/s and 10 m/s, the faster one moves at 5 m/s east relative to the slower one.

Write a positive direction before using signed positions, velocities, or accelerations. A negative displacement describes direction, not a negative path length.

08 Average & instantaneous speed and velocity
Average speed = total distance / total elapsed time
Average velocity = displacement / total elapsed time
Instantaneous velocity: v = dr/dt
Instantaneous speed = |v|

Average speed is not generally the magnitude of average velocity. They coincide when the path is straight with no reversal. For a completed round trip, average velocity is zero while average speed is positive.

Uniform velocity requires constant magnitude and direction. Constant speed alone is insufficient: circular motion changes direction continuously. Instantaneous velocity is tangent to the path.

Equal-distance example: Cover the first half of a journey at 10 m/s and the second half at 20 m/s. Choose 100 m for each half: total distance = 200 m, total time = 10 + 5 = 15 s. Average speed = 13.33 m/s, not 15 m/s. For equal time intervals, the arithmetic mean would apply.

Speed and velocity both use m/s and have dimensions LT⁻¹. A graph of position against time gives instantaneous velocity from the tangent slope.

09 Acceleration, signs & changing direction
aaverage = Δv/Δt
a = dv/dt = d²r/dt²

Acceleration is a vector; its SI unit is m/s² and its dimensions are LT⁻². It can result from a change in speed, direction, or both. Uniform acceleration means its magnitude and direction remain constant.

Sign rule in one dimension: Speed increases when velocity and acceleration have the same sign. Speed decreases when their signs are opposite. Negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down.

Examples: With east positive, v = +8 m/s and a = −2 m/s² describe slowing eastward motion. With v = −8 m/s and a = −2 m/s², the object moves west and speeds up. A released object can have initial velocity zero while its acceleration is nonzero.

Uniform acceleration need not imply a straight path: ideal projectile motion has constant downward acceleration while its trajectory is curved. Component equations handle that two-dimensional motion.

A horizontal position–time line means rest. A straight sloping line means constant velocity. A curve with increasing positive slope means increasing positive velocity. A tangent slope gives an instantaneous value; the slope between two points gives an average.

Area below the time axis is negative displacement. To find distance, add magnitudes of the separate areas, or integrate speed. For +3 m/s over 2 s then −3 m/s over 2 s, displacement is zero and distance is 12 m.

11 Three equations of uniformly accelerated motion

For motion along one axis with constant acceleration, let u be initial velocity, v final velocity, t elapsed time, and s signed displacement.

v = u + at
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as
s = ½(u + v)t
  1. First equation: a = (v − u)/t gives v = u + at.
  2. Second equation: Under a straight-line velocity–time graph, displacement is rectangle + triangle: s = ut + ½(v − u)t. Substitute v − u = at to get s = ut + ½at².
  3. Third equation: The same area is ½(u + v)t. Substitute t = (v − u)/a to get s = (v² − u²)/(2a), hence v² = u² + 2as. The equation also holds when a = 0.

Choose the equation: Use v² = u² + 2as when time is absent. Use s = ut + ½at² when final velocity is absent. Apply these component by component for constant vector acceleration.

These formulas do not generally apply with a changing acceleration. Convert minutes to seconds and kilometres to metres before substituting.

12 Worked motion problems & checking answers

From rest

A body starts from rest and accelerates at 3 m/s² for 4 s. v = 0 + 3(4) = 12 m/s. s = 0 + ½(3)(4²) = 24 m. Check: average velocity = (0 + 12)/2 = 6 m/s; multiplying by 4 s also gives 24 m.

Stopping distance

A car moving at 20 m/s brakes with constant acceleration −5 m/s². At rest, v = 0. Time: 0 = 20 − 5t, so t = 4 s. Distance: 0² = 20² + 2(−5)s, so s = 40 m. The car has not reversed during braking, so displacement magnitude equals distance.

Distance in the nth second

For constant acceleration, subtract the displacement at t = n − 1 s from that at t = n s. The displacement during that one-second interval is u(1 s) + ½a[(n s)² − ((n−1)s)²]. Numerically in SI units, it is u + ½a(2n−1). From rest at 2 m/s², the fifth-second displacement is 9 m, while total displacement in five seconds is 25 m.

“In five seconds” and “during the fifth second” ask different questions. If motion reverses during an interval, signed displacement alone does not give path length.

13 Free fall & vertical motion

Free fall is motion under gravity alone. Near the Earth's surface, take g ≈ 9.8 m/s² unless the question specifies a rounded value. Ignore air resistance and choose a sign convention.

Upward positive: a = −g
v = u − gt
y − y₀ = ut − ½gt²
v² = u² − 2g(y − y₀)

A dropped body has u = 0; a body thrown upward has positive u. At the top its vertical velocity is momentarily zero, but acceleration remains −g. For return to the same height, ascent and descent times are equal in this ideal model.

Worked example (g = 10 m/s²): Throw a ball vertically upward at 20 m/s. Time to top = u/g = 2 s. Rise = u²/(2g) = 20 m. Total time back to launch height = 4 s. Its returning velocity is −20 m/s.

Release from a moving carrier: A package released from a rising helicopter initially has the helicopter's upward velocity. It is not “dropped from rest” relative to the ground. Use its actual initial velocity and solve for the time at which it reaches ground level.

14 Projectile motion: independent components

An ideal projectile moves under uniform downward gravity with negligible air resistance, near the Earth's surface. Resolve launch speed u at angle θ above horizontal:

ux = u cos θ   uy = u sin θ
ax = 0   ay = −g
x = x₀ + (u cos θ)t
y = y₀ + (u sin θ)t − ½gt²
vx = u cos θ   vy = u sin θ − gt

The same time t appears in both components. Horizontal motion is uniform; vertical motion has constant acceleration. At the highest point, vy = 0, but vx usually is not zero. A vertically launched object is the special case with ux = 0.

Eliminate t = (x − x₀)/(u cos θ), when cos θ ≠ 0:

y − y₀ = (x − x₀)tan θ − g(x − x₀)²/(2u²cos²θ)

This quadratic relation gives a parabolic trajectory. If two objects start at the same height with the same initial vertical velocity, one launched horizontally and one released vertically hit the ground together in this ideal model.

15 Time of flight, maximum height & range

Time to the highest point

Set vy = 0 in vy = u sin θ − gt:

tup = u sin θ/g

Time of flight to the same height

Set y − y₀ = 0. The nonzero root of t(u sin θ − ½gt) = 0 is:

T = 2u sin θ/g

Maximum rise above the launch point

Use vy² = uy² − 2gH with vy = 0:

H = u²sin²θ/(2g)

Horizontal range to the same height

R = uxT. Use 2 sin θ cos θ = sin 2θ:

R = u²sin 2θ/g

At fixed u and g, range is maximum at θ = 45° and Rmax = u²/g. Complementary angles θ and 90° − θ give the same range, but the higher angle gives a longer flight and greater height.

The T and R formulas above require equal launch and landing heights. For a cliff or platform, solve the vertical position equation for positive time first, then multiply by ux.

16 Worked projectiles & chapter exercise methods

Launch at an angle

With u = 20 m/s, θ = 30°, and g = 10 m/s²: ux = 10√3 m/s; uy = 10 m/s. Thus tup = 1 s; T = 2 s; H = 5 m; R = 20√3 ≈ 34.64 m, assuming landing at launch height.

Horizontal launch

Launch horizontally at 12 m/s from a height of 20 m, with g = 10 m/s². Vertical initial velocity is zero, so −20 = −½(10)t² gives t = 2 s. Horizontal range from the base of the platform is 12 × 2 = 24 m. Final vertical velocity is −20 m/s.

Unequal heights: a rising helicopter

A package is released 80 m above ground while rising at 12 m/s. With upward positive and g = 9.8 m/s², 0 = 80 + 12t − 4.9t². The positive root is (12 + √1712)/9.8 ≈ 5.45 s. The initial upward motion delays impact.

Exercise relationships

For equal-level projectile motion, R/H = 4 cot θ. If R = H, tan θ = 4 and θ ≈ 76°. When two perpendicular nonzero vectors are compared, the dot product is zero and the cross-product magnitude is AB. If the scalar dot product equals the cross-product magnitude for an acute angle, cos θ = sin θ, giving 45°.

Two possible launch angles: Given R and u at equal heights, sin 2θ = Rg/u² can yield complementary angles. Check both unless a low or high trajectory is specified.

Air resistance changes the ideal trajectory and range, so the ideal 45° maximum and equal-height symmetry should not be assumed when drag matters. An aircraft powered in flight is not an ideal projectile.
